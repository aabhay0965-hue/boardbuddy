Introduction

Agriculture is the backbone of the Indian economy. It includes growing crops, rearing animals, and producing raw materials for food and industries. Nearly half of India’s population depends on agriculture for livelihood.

Types of Farming in India

1. Primitive Subsistence Farming

  • Practised on small patches of land.
  • Depends on monsoon and natural fertility.
  • Traditional tools are used.

2. Intensive Subsistence Farming

  • High use of labour and inputs.
  • Small land holdings.
  • Mainly for local consumption.

3. Commercial Farming

  • Production for sale in markets.
  • Use of machines and fertilizers.
  • High yield crops.

Major Cropping Seasons in India

Kharif Season

  • Sown in June–July (monsoon).
  • Harvested in September–October.
  • Examples: Rice, maize, cotton.

Rabi Season

  • Sown in October–December.
  • Harvested in March–April.
  • Examples: Wheat, barley, mustard.

Zaid Season

  • Short season between Rabi and Kharif.
  • Examples: Watermelon, cucumber.

Importance of Agriculture

  • Provides food to the population.
  • Supplies raw materials to industries.
  • Generates employment.
  • Earns foreign exchange through exports.

NCERT Insight

Agriculture in India is heavily dependent on monsoon, which makes it vulnerable to climate variations and uneven rainfall distribution.

Major Crops in India

India grows a wide variety of crops due to diverse climate, soil types, and rainfall patterns. Crops are mainly classified into food crops and cash crops.

1. Rice

  • Climate: High temperature and high rainfall
  • Soil: Clayey and alluvial soil
  • Regions: West Bengal, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu
  • Importance: Staple food for a large population

2. Wheat

  • Climate: Cool growing season and bright sunshine during ripening
  • Soil: Fertile alluvial soil
  • Regions: Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh
  • Importance: Second most important food crop

3. Millets (Jowar, Bajra, Ragi)

  • Climate: Dry and less fertile regions
  • Regions: Rajasthan, Karnataka, Maharashtra
  • Importance: Nutritious coarse grains, drought resistant

Cash Crops

1. Cotton

  • Climate: Hot and dry climate with moderate rainfall
  • Soil: Black soil (Regur soil)
  • Regions: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana
  • Use: Textile industry

2. Jute

  • Climate: Hot and humid climate
  • Soil: Alluvial soil
  • Regions: West Bengal, Bihar, Assam
  • Use: Rope, sacks, packaging material

3. Tea

  • Climate: Cool climate with heavy rainfall
  • Soil: Well-drained acidic soil
  • Regions: Assam, West Bengal (Darjeeling), Tamil Nadu
  • Use: Beverage crop

4. Coffee

  • Climate: Warm and humid with shade trees
  • Regions: Karnataka, Kerala
  • Use: Beverage crop

Green Revolution

The Green Revolution refers to the rapid increase in agricultural production during the 1960s and 1970s due to modern farming techniques.

Main Features

  • Use of high-yielding variety (HYV) seeds.
  • Increased use of fertilizers and pesticides.
  • Improved irrigation facilities.
  • Mechanisation of farming.

Impact

  • Increase in food grain production.
  • Improved food security in India.
  • Regional imbalance in development.

NCERT Insight

The Green Revolution transformed Indian agriculture, but it also created environmental concerns like soil degradation and water overuse.

Agricultural Development in India

Agricultural development in India has moved from traditional methods to modern, technology-based farming. The main goal is to increase productivity while ensuring sustainability.

Key Developments

  • Introduction of HYV seeds during the Green Revolution.
  • Expansion of irrigation facilities.
  • Use of fertilizers and pesticides.
  • Mechanisation of agriculture.

Subsistence vs Commercial Farming

Subsistence Farming

  • Done for family consumption.
  • Small land holdings.
  • Low technology use.
  • Depends heavily on monsoon.

Commercial Farming

  • Done for market sale.
  • Large farms and high investment.
  • Use of machines and modern inputs.
  • High productivity.

Irrigation Methods in India

Irrigation is the artificial supply of water to crops when rainfall is insufficient.

Major Types of Irrigation

  • Canal Irrigation: Water is supplied through canals from rivers.
  • Well and Tube Well Irrigation: Groundwater is used for farming.
  • Tank Irrigation: Rainwater is stored in artificial tanks.

Problems Faced by Indian Agriculture

  • Dependence on monsoon rainfall.
  • Small and fragmented land holdings.
  • Low productivity in many regions.
  • Lack of modern technology in rural areas.
  • Debt and financial stress on farmers.

Modern Challenges in Agriculture

  • Excessive use of chemical fertilizers leading to soil degradation.
  • Groundwater depletion due to over-irrigation.
  • Climate change affecting crop yield.
  • Rising cost of farming inputs.
  • Loss of soil fertility in many regions.

NCERT Insight

Indian agriculture is evolving, but balancing productivity with environmental sustainability remains the biggest challenge.
